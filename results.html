Case study 1 · IT operations transformation

Turning around a complex healthcare IT operation

At a glance: Complex healthcare IT operation for a client · full IT operations across multiple service towers · January 2025 to April 2026 · My role: delivery lead and client partner, accountable for the program
Headline result: achieved a 92.1% reduction in quarterly production outages, from 38 in the first quarter of the transformation to just 3 in the final quarter, across a portfolio of business applications and IT operational services, through improved operational governance, proactive monitoring, and systematic root-cause remediation.

The challenge

  • No formal SLAs, missing metrics, and inconsistently used tools
  • Delivery that depended on tribal knowledge and a few key individuals
  • A 1,274-item aged backlog and 38 production outages in the program’s first quarter, a sign of constant firefighting
  • A mandate to stabilize business-critical production while building a repeatable delivery model

What I did

  • Stabilize: stood up a 24x7 NOC, L1, L2, L3 triage, and production deployment guardrails
  • Standardize: put ITSM and ITIL and a software delivery lifecycle in place, with SOPs, a service catalog, and baselined SLAs and KPIs; trained 100% of staff on ITSM
  • Automate: analyzed 16,968 incident tickets, found about 30% automatable, and deployed alert correlation, routing, and job monitoring
  • Modernize: moved delivery to a modern work-tracking system with Agile practice, and built the disaster-recovery and secure-development runway

Case study 2 · Application portfolio assessment

Rationalizing a 350-application portfolio into a funded, sequenced roadmap

At a glance: Large healthcare organization · about 350 applications across three business segments · My role: program lead · Outcome: executive buy-in for the first wave of a three-wave, multi-year investment roadmap

The challenge

Leadership needed one fact base for deciding where to invest, where to consolidate, and what to retire across a large portfolio that had grown over many years.

The approach, at a high level

  1. See it. Inventory the portfolio and map each application to the business capability it supports.
  2. Judge it. Assess business value and technical fit on one consistent scale, so the conversation moves from opinion to evidence.
  3. Cost it. Build a total-cost-of-ownership view for each application.
  4. Decide it. Give every application a disposition from a standard set of six options, then sequence the work into waves leadership can fund one at a time.

What I delivered

  • An application-to-capability map
  • A business and technical fit assessment
  • A total-cost baseline
  • A disposition for every application
  • A three-wave, multi-year investment roadmap
  • An executive readout that secured sponsor buy-in for Wave 1

The financial view

ItemResult
Total-cost baselineMore than $28 million across the portfolio, built application by application
Investment structureThree waves over about three years, sequenced so leadership can fund one at a time
Decision securedExecutive sponsor buy-in for Wave 1
Why it matters: A portfolio you can’t see is a portfolio you can’t govern, whether the pressure is cost, risk, audit, or an acquisition. The deliverable is a decision, not a report that sits on a shelf. I share the detailed method when we scope your engagement.
Case study 3 · Enterprise platform

One shared enterprise platform that business users largely run themselves

At a glance: Large healthcare organization with a national member network · enterprise Salesforce platform serving member organizations nationwide and internal business units · My role: led the platform’s growth and governance through influence, without positional authority
Headline result: grew the enterprise platform from one solution to ten over three years, on shared licensing, reuse, and a common security model, and set it up so business users could handle about 80% of the changes their production solutions needed, with no IT development queue.

The challenge

  • Serve two audiences from one platform: solutions exposed to member organizations across the country, and enterprise solutions used by internal business units
  • Keep demand disciplined by separating real business needs from wants
  • Avoid duplicate solutions, duplicate licenses, and a different security model for every project
  • Automate complex, multi-step business workflows that touch regulated data
  • Build adoption with no positional authority over the teams that had to adopt it

What I did

  • Needs before wants: every request was tested against the business outcome it had to deliver, and the solution was sized to the need
  • Reuse first: shared components, data models, and patterns before any new build
  • Secure access by design: one governed access model for external and internal users, so a new solution inherited the controls instead of reinventing them
  • Shared licenses: pooled licensing across solutions in place of project-by-project purchases
  • Low-code and no-code first: configuration over custom code, so routine change belonged to the business rather than a development backlog
  • Workflow automation: complex approval, case, and service workflows automated on the platform

The platform footprint

Service Cloud, Community Cloud, Sales Cloud, Marketing Cloud, the Lightning Platform, and CRM, used for national-scale service and engagement solutions as well as internal enterprise solutions.

The outcomes

AreaBeforeAfter
Solutions on the shared platform110, over three years
Production changes handled by business usersnot reportedAbout 80% of the changes needed
Licensing and security modelper projectShared and governed
Why it matters: A platform pays off when it is shared. Disciplined intake, reuse, common security, and business-owned change keep cost and risk down while adoption grows. The same pattern fits any regulated organization running several business units on one platform.
Case study 4 · Migration readiness

Migration readiness and cutover risk reduction on a core healthcare platform

At a glance: Complex healthcare IT operation for a client · legacy core system to a modern platform · eight-plus vendors, with a global consulting firm running the program office · 2025 to 2026 · My role: owner of the vendor testing process, change control, and key delivery milestones
This case study covers readiness, decision framing, and cutover planning, which is the part of the program I owned. Post-go-live program results are not reported here.

The challenge

  • Move core transaction processing to the new platform across a multi-vendor ecosystem
  • A spring pilot go-live ahead of a larger mid-year go-live
  • A timeline compressed by roughly two months after contract finalization paused work already under way
  • Real risk to live member and customer transactions

What I did

  • Framed the decision for leadership as two risk-mitigated go-live options instead of one high-risk cutover: phased enablement with parallel, dark-mode testing, or a controlled run-out on the legacy system with a clean cutover
  • Defined date-of-service cutover logic so business teams could validate outcomes before live processing
  • Owned the vendor testing process and change control across every partner
  • Sequenced the core interfaces to protect member operations

What I delivered

  • A clear, defensible go-live recommendation that minimized disruption and production risk
  • Date-of-service and run-out logic the business could test before it went live
  • A coordinated multi-vendor test-and-cutover plan under an externally compressed schedule
